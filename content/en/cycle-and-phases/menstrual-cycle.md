## Glance
The menstrual cycle is the monthly rhythm your body runs on to prepare for a possible pregnancy.

## Overview
The menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of your period — that's day 1 — up to the day
before your next period starts. It's driven by hormones that rise and fall in a repeating pattern
each month.

A cycle is often around 28 days, but that's just an average. Anywhere from about 21 to 35 days is
normal, and your own length can vary a little from month to month. Learning your own pattern is
more useful than comparing it to a number.

Across the cycle your body moves through a few stages: your period, the build-up to releasing an
egg, the release itself (ovulation), and the stretch afterwards. Each stage is shaped by
hormones, mainly estrogen and progesterone.

## Deep
The whole cycle is a conversation between the brain and the ovaries, carried by hormones. In the
first half, estrogen rises as an egg-containing follicle matures in the ovary and the lining of
the uterus (womb) thickens. Around the middle of the cycle an egg is released. In the second half,
progesterone helps keep that lining ready in case a fertilised egg arrives.

If there's no pregnancy, estrogen and progesterone fall to low levels, the lining is shed as your
period, and the pattern begins again. Because these hormones touch many systems, you may notice
small day-to-day changes across the month — this is the normal rhythm of the cycle, not something
to "fix."
