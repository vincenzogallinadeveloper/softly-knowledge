## Glance
Coercive control is a pattern of controlling behaviour — isolating, monitoring, threatening — that is a recognised form of abuse.

## Overview
Coercive control is a form of abuse that works through control rather than, or as well as, physical
harm. It is a pattern — not a single argument — in which one person gradually limits another's
freedom and sense of self.

It can look like being cut off from friends and family, having your phone, movements or spending
watched or restricted, being told what to wear or how to behave, or living with constant criticism,
threats, or intimidation. Because there may be no bruises, it can be hard to name, and others may
not see it — but it is recognised as abuse, and it can be just as harmful as physical violence.

If you recognise some of this, it is not a sign that you are too sensitive or that you brought it on
yourself. Controlling behaviour is a choice the other person makes. Understanding it for what it is
can be the first step toward support.

## Deep
Coercive control often builds slowly, which is part of what makes it hard to spot from the inside —
small controls become normal, and self-doubt grows. It wears on mental health over time. Talking it
through with someone trained to understand these patterns can help you see the situation more
clearly and think about what you want to do next, without pressure.

## When to see a doctor
- An anti-violence centre or helpline (in Italy, **1522**) can talk through what you're experiencing,
  confidentially and free of charge, and help you understand your options — you don't have to be in
  physical danger to reach out.
- A GP or counsellor can also support you and point you toward specialist services.

## Red flags
- If you are in **immediate danger**, contact emergency services now: **112** in the EU (including
  Italy), **999** in the UK, **911** in the US and Canada.
- In Italy, **1522** is free, available 24 hours a day, anonymous, and multilingual — by phone or
  chat.
