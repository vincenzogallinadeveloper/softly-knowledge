## Glance
Taking the pill with shorter breaks, fewer breaks or none means bleeding less often, or not at all. It's something to discuss with a clinician.

## Overview
The combined pill is often taken for 21 days followed by a 7-day break, or as an "everyday" pack that
includes a few dummy pills. During the break, or the dummy pills, you usually have a bleed. It looks
like a period, but it's caused by the drop in hormones when you pause the pill, rather than by your
natural cycle.

Some people take the combined pill in a "tailored" way instead: with a shorter break, fewer breaks, or
no break between packs. This can mean bleeding less often, or not at all, and it can ease symptoms
that come with the break, such as headaches and bleeding. Whether it suits you depends on you and on
your type of pill, so it's worth talking it through with a doctor, nurse or pharmacist.

The progestogen-only pill is different: it's taken every day, with no break. It, the hormonal coil
(IUS) and the implant can all change your periods: they may become lighter or irregular, or stop
altogether.

## Deep
When periods stop because of a hormonal method, that's an expected effect of the hormones rather than
a sign that something is wrong. Light bleeding or spotting is common, especially in the first months
of a new method or a new way of taking it, and it often settles with time. If bleeding carries on,
a clinician can explain the options — they may suggest a short break, or a different pill or method.

For some conditions where periods bring a lot of pain or heavy bleeding — such as endometriosis,
adenomyosis, or very painful or heavy periods — clinicians may suggest hormonal options that make
periods lighter or stop them, and taking the pill with fewer breaks or none can be one of them.
Hormones can ease symptoms, but they don't cure these conditions.

With no regular bleed, there's no cycle to follow, but noting pain, bleeding and other symptoms still
shows how you're doing, and it's useful to bring to appointments. How well the pill protects against
pregnancy still depends on taking it as advised; the leaflet with your pills, or a pharmacist, explains
what to do if you miss one.

## When to see a doctor
It's worth talking to a doctor, nurse or pharmacist if:

- you'd like to take your pill with fewer breaks or none, or change how you take it
- bleeding or spotting carries on, gets heavier, or bothers you (with the hormonal coil, irregular
  bleeding that lasts more than about 6 months is worth checking)
- you bleed after sex, or have new pain in your lower tummy
- you've missed pills and had sex, or think you could be pregnant
- the pain or other symptoms you take hormones for come back or get worse

## Red flags
- The combined pill slightly raises the risk of a **blood clot**. Get **urgent** medical help if you have
  **pain and swelling in one leg**, or warm, red or darkened skin on the leg.
- Call **{{emergency}}** or go to the emergency department straight away if you have **chest pain**,
  **sudden breathlessness** or **cough up blood** — these can be signs of a clot in the lungs.
