## Glance
If someone you know may be experiencing abuse, listening without judgement — and knowing where to point them — helps.

## Overview
Realising that someone you care about may be experiencing abuse is hard, and wanting to help is a
good instinct. The most useful things you can offer are often the simplest.

Listen, and believe them. Let them talk at their own pace, without judging, blaming, or asking what
they did to cause it. Say plainly that it is not their fault and that they don't deserve it. Try not
to tell them what to do or push them to leave: leaving is theirs to decide, and doing it in a hurry
can actually raise the danger — what helps more is that they feel supported and not alone.

Keep what they tell you private. Gently help them find specialist support when they're ready — a
helpline like **1522** in Italy, or a local anti-violence centre — and offer practical help, like
looking up numbers together or being there when they call. And look after yourself too; supporting
someone through this can be heavy.

## When to see a doctor
- Encourage them (without pressure) to contact a helpline or anti-violence centre, who can guide both
  of you on what to do next. You can offer to help them make the call or find the number.
- If they are injured, a GP or hospital can help — and connect them with support services.

## Red flags
- If they are in **immediate danger**, call emergency services: **112** in the EU (including Italy),
  **999** in the UK, **911** in the US and Canada.
- In Italy, **1522** (free, 24/7, anonymous, multilingual — phone or chat) can advise you on how to
  help someone, too.
