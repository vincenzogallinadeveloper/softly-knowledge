## Glance
Endometriosis is a condition where tissue like the womb lining grows outside the womb, often causing pain.

## Overview
Endometriosis is a long-term condition where cells similar to the lining of the womb (uterus) grow
in other parts of the body, such as around the ovaries, the pelvis, or the bowel. It's common —
estimated to affect around 1 in 10 women of reproductive age worldwide.

Because this tissue behaves a little like the womb lining, it can cause inflammation and, over time,
scar tissue. The most common sign is pain: often severe period pain, but also pelvic or lower-back
pain at other times, pain during or after sex, and pain when peeing or pooing. Very heavy periods,
deep tiredness, low mood, and difficulty getting pregnant are also common. Symptoms vary a lot —
some people have severe pain, others little.

Endometriosis is often misunderstood and can take years to recognise, partly because period pain is
so often dismissed as "normal." Severe pain that disrupts your life is not something you simply have
to put up with — it's worth taking seriously.

## Deep
The exact cause of endometriosis isn't known, and there's currently no cure — but treatments can
help manage the symptoms, and support ranges from medicines to, for some people, surgery, alongside
help for the physical and emotional toll. One of the biggest challenges is delay: on average it can
take several years, sometimes many, to reach a diagnosis. That's part of why keeping a clear record
of your symptoms, and returning to a clinician if you're not being heard, can matter so much.

## When to see a doctor
It's worth seeing a GP if:

- you have severe period pain that stops you doing normal activities
- your periods are very heavy — for example, needing to change a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours
- you have ongoing pelvic or lower-back pain, pain during or after sex, or pain when peeing or pooing
- you're finding it hard to get pregnant
- treatment you've already had hasn't helped, or your symptoms have got worse

Because endometriosis can take time to diagnose, it's okay to go back if your symptoms aren't taken
seriously — a clear picture of your pain over time helps. If the pain or heavy bleeding is also
affecting your mood, tell your GP; that's part of the picture, and support is available.
