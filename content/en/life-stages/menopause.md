## Glance
Menopause is when periods stop for good — a natural stage, usually reached between about 45 and 55.

## Overview
Menopause is the point when your periods stop permanently. Because periods can come and go for a
while first, it's counted as reached once you've gone 12 months in a row without one. It usually
happens between about 45 and 55, though it can be earlier or later.

It's a natural part of ageing, not an illness. It happens as the ovaries wind down and the level of
estrogen — a key hormone of the cycle — falls. That hormonal shift is behind the changes many
people notice: hot flushes and night sweats, changes to periods in the lead-up, trouble sleeping,
shifts in mood or anxiety, brain fog, vaginal dryness, and aches.

Experiences vary enormously — some people sail through, others find it hard, and symptoms can last
several years. None of it means something is wrong with you. Support and options exist, and
understanding what's happening is a good first step.

## Deep
The years of change leading up to menopause are called perimenopause; menopause itself is a single
point in time (that 12-months-without-a-period mark), and the time after is sometimes called
postmenopause. Because estrogen also affects bones and the heart over the long term, clinicians pay
attention to those alongside day-to-day symptoms. A range of approaches — from lifestyle to
non-hormonal and hormonal treatments — can ease symptoms, and what's right depends on your health,
history and preferences, which is a conversation to have with a clinician.

## When to see a doctor
- See a GP if menopause symptoms are troubling you or affecting daily life, and you'd like to talk
  through your options.
- **Always** see a GP if you have **any vaginal bleeding after 12 months without a period**
  (bleeding after menopause). It's often nothing serious, but it should always be checked.
- It's also worth checking in if you're bleeding more rather than less than before, or your periods
  change in a way that worries you.
